# Which games are worth this: the full list

The README shows eight. This is the longer answer.

**This is not a "best story games" list.** A game earns its place here not by being great, but by meeting a narrower condition: **playing it this way makes the first experience significantly better.** Plenty of great games fail that condition (see the last group), and some less-than-great games pass it.

The test is the three things this tool actually does. Owning one of them outright is enough:

- **Give** — the original audience got context for free; missing it, today's player can't read the game.
- **Guard** — the work treats *when you learn what* as a design object; scramble the order and the work is damaged.
- **Read** — the text and allusions are dense enough that you keep wanting someone to talk to, about exactly the screen in front of you.

House rules:

- **Reasons never touch content.** No line below tells you any plot — including the words "there's a twist."
- **A reason that survives a game-name swap doesn't get written.** Every line is true of that one game only.
- **Series are rated per entry; remakes rated separately.** Same series, entirely different information environment.
- **Being listed is itself a faint spoiler.** "It's on this list" is already one bit of information. That's the built-in cost of any list like this. We accept it — in exchange, you learn nothing else.
- **"Back then" has a language.** The same game can have a thick English-side archive and a thin Chinese-side one, or the reverse. When the period can't be dug up, it tells you at the start instead of inventing one.

The list is alive. If your game isn't here, just give it the name. If you think something should be added or dropped, [argue in an issue](https://github.com/martinsin0223/the-fool/issues).

## 1. It hands the year back to you

These run on **give**. Without that year's air, these games read wrong today.

| Game | Year | Why it's worth playing this way |
|---|---|---|
| Doom | 1993 | The genre name "FPS" only became necessary after it. Its first episode was free and crawled across the world's computers by floppy disk and phone line — an event that can't be rerun, but the period discussions survive |
| EarthBound | 1995 | Its official American ad campaign was "this game stinks" (complete with scratch-and-sniff cards), and it genuinely bombed. A game killed by its own marketing, then slowly acquitted over the next twenty years — the failure is part of the work |
| Chrono Trigger | 1995 | "The father of Dragon Quest × the father of Final Fantasy × Toriyama" — in 1995 that lineup was an event in itself. Players pressed Start carrying the question *what do these three make together?* That question is still worth carrying |
| Sword and Fairy (仙剑奇侠传) | 1995 | The game that taught a generation of Chinese players that games can make you cry, and the era's answer to whether Chinese-language games could tell stories at all. Its launch-era record lives almost entirely in Chinese — a game whose "back then" exists in one language |
| Resident Evil | 1996 | The term "survival horror" was invented for it. Tank controls and fixed cameras read as dated today; back then they were a recipe for refining inconvenience into fear. Standing in 1996 is how you tell the limitations from the design |
| Pokémon Red/Green | 1996 | Half of its "back then" happened at recess: whether Mew is under that truck, the saturated ecosystem of playground legend — a thing that could only exist in schoolyards without internet. Those rumors are all on record, and half of them beat the truth |
| Heroes of Jin Yong (金庸群侠传) | 1996 | The phrase "open world" didn't exist in 1996; this had already spread out a whole jianghu for you to wander, join masters, betray them. Its freedom reads as crude today — unless you know what everything else looked like that year. Its record lives in Chinese |
| Super Mario 64 | 1996 | Every 3D game's sense of movement descends from it. To feel what 1996 felt — pushing an analog stick for the first time, watching Mario run in an arc — you first need *what 3D looked like before* installed |
| Final Fantasy VII | 1997 | It landed on the industry's hinge. Not knowing what came after is how you feel what people were losing their minds about |
| Half-Life | 1998 | It proved an FPS could tell its story without cutting away — after the opening tram ride, the whole genre changed its gait. Every shooter today has its skeleton, which is exactly why you can't see what it invented |
| The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time | 1998 | It defined the grammar of 3D adventure games, and that grammar is now air. You have to stand in the year it was new to see why it detonated |
| Shenmue | 1999 | The most expensive game ever made at the time, with a platform holder's fate riding on it. Punishing to play today — unless you know what it promised in 1999, and how insane "living a daily life inside a game" sounded back then |
| The Legend of Zelda: Majora's Mask | 2000 | Players were waiting for "more Ocarina" and got something else entirely — the bewilderment is on record, and worth feeling freshly. A sequel refusing to act like one was, in 2000, a small crisis of trust |
| Deus Ex | 2000 | The complete anthology of millennium conspiracy culture — Area 51, black helicopters, MJ12, all that X-Files-era air. After 9/11, treating conspiracy theories as entertainment lost its lightness for good; this game preserves the last year it was possible |
| Grand Theft Auto III | 2001 | An open 3D city didn't exist in 2001, and the panic it set off is real history: TV debates, lawyers, legislation. Buying it carried a whiff of contraband — a flavor nobody can mix anymore |
| Metal Gear Solid 2 | 2001 | For two years before release, every trailer was studied frame by frame; thousands bought a different game entirely for the demo disc inside it. How thick that wait was, and what exactly everyone was waiting for, nobody remembers today — and all of it is on the record |
| The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind | 2002 | It gives you written directions, not quest markers. Today that's called friction; back then it was called travel. Playing it means uninstalling habits that only grew into players' heads after 2006 — which happens to be somebody's job now |
| Star Wars: Knights of the Old Republic | 2003 | In 2003 it was writing into the actual Star Wars canon; a decade later that entire universe was officially voided. Playing it is visiting a demolished timeline — and players back then believed they stood inside the real one. Borrow that belief |
| Metal Gear Solid 3 | 2004 | Its ending asks to hit you while you're defenseless; know it's coming and the punch lands on nothing. It's also a prequel — players in 2004 walked in carrying two games' worth of questions, and those should be handed back to you first |
| Half-Life 2 | 2004 | Its physics engine was a spectacle in 2004 and is air today. Add the six-year wait, the most famous source-code leak in history, and a launch week when the whole internet was cursing Steam — the release itself is a historical site worth visiting |
| Trails in the Sky FC | 2004 | Its ending is one of the most vicious cliffhangers in the genre's history. Japanese players hung on it for two years; English players waited four and a half more for the sequel's translation. You can hang for just one night — but you should know what kind of wait you're skipping |
| Xuan-Yuan Sword: The Scar of Sky (苍之涛) | 2004 | Not the best-seller of its year; slowly crowned the series' finest over the two decades since. "Missed by its own era" is its outer story — you can choose to be the 2004 player who didn't miss it. Its record, too, lives almost entirely in Chinese |
| Shadow of the Colossus | 2005 | In 2005, "are games art" was still a live courtroom fight, and this was the exhibit both sides subpoenaed. Walk in carrying that fight and every silence in it gets louder |
| MOTHER 3 | 2006 | Still no official English release; the English-speaking world plays it through one famous fan translation. The twenty-year petition — "it shouldn't stay in Japan" — is part of its history. A game whose "back then" hasn't ended yet |
| Dark Souls | 2011 | In 2011 nobody knew what a "soulslike" was; the wiki was mortared together stone by stone by the whole internet. Today you know it through memes and tributes — players then had one line, "Prepare to Die," and the dark |
| Gone Home | 2013 | It detonated the "is this even a game" fight of 2013, and that fight is half its context. The other half is how precisely it uses the assumptions you walk in with — and the 2013 set of assumptions is hard to grow naturally now |
| Bloodborne | 2015 | In March 2015 the whole world assumed it was Dark Souls in a new coat. That misunderstanding is worth a lot, and worth receiving in full |
| Metal Gear Solid V: The Phantom Pain | 2015 | Its announcement was an official hoax: a fake studio, a fake developer, and months of worldwide detective work before the unmasking. Those months exist only in 2012–2013 — chapter zero of this work isn't in the game, it's in the historical record |

## 2. It guards what you don't know yet

These run on **guard**. Their being listed is the faint spoiler admitted in the preamble — the cost gets paid in this group.

| Game | Year | Why it's worth playing this way |
|---|---|---|
| Silent Hill 2 | 2001 | Its center is an understanding you have to walk into yourself. Hand over the conclusion and the walk is empty |
| Phoenix Wright: Ace Attorney | 2001 | Each case is worth exactly one playthrough. And its guide contamination is bad enough that searching any character's name can put the verdict in the first line of results |
| Braid | 2008 | The opening statement of the "indie games can be serious" movement — a thing that still needed proving in 2008. And its puzzles and its point are the same object: a walkthrough doesn't kill the difficulty, it kills the meaning |
| Nine Hours, Nine Persons, Nine Doors (999) | 2009 | It was written for one specific dual-screen machine, whose role in the telling no port can fully restore. A game where picking the right version *is* spoiler management — and no store page will tell you that |
| FEZ | 2012 | On the surface, a gentle puzzle game; its deepest puzzles were cracked collectively, by the whole internet, in 2012. You can't reproduce that archaeology alone — but once you finish, someone should tell you what happened behind the walls you walked past |
| Spec Ops: The Line | 2012 | Nothing can be said about it, including why it's worth playing. It's the extreme case of meta-spoiler damage — its presence on this list is everything I can tell you |
| The House in Fata Morgana | 2012 | Its community holds exactly one consensus: look nothing up. A work its own fans placed in a safe — you don't lack protection, you lack someone safe to ask |
| SOMA | 2015 | It turns an old thought experiment into your first-person situation. Being told the conclusion in advance is just being told — the entire point is being the subject yourself |
| Undertale | 2015 | The entire design assumes this is your first time. Any preparation makes it stop working on you |
| INSIDE | 2016 | Not one line of dialogue in the whole game; the assembly is yours to do. Let someone assemble it for you and there's nothing left |
| The Witness | 2016 | It teaches you a language, and the learning is the entire content. Looking up one puzzle means letting someone else learn it for you |
| Doki Doki Literature Club | 2017 | Its store page carries an official warning. That warning is everything you're permitted to know |
| NieR: Automata | 2017 | Guide contamination so total that even "am I done yet?" is a spoilable question. What it needs isn't a wiki — it's someone who knows your progress and answers only about what's in front of you |
| Hollow Knight | 2017 | Its wiki has no safe page: look up one charm and the page casually names three late-game areas. And one day you *will* want to look up a charm |
| Return of the Obra Dinn | 2018 | Deduction is the only content there is. Looking up one answer isn't a shortcut — it deletes that stretch of the game |
| Outer Wilds | 2019 | Your only progress bar is what you know. Whatever gets spoiled is gone for good; replaying won't get it back |
| 13 Sentinels: Aegis Rim | 2019 | The order in which thirteen viewpoints hand you information is the entire design. It may be the game on this list most afraid of being *described* — even praise leaks |
| Tunic | 2022 | It turns the instruction manual into the core mechanic; every page you find rewrites the rules you thought you knew. Looking anything up is reading someone else's manual |

## 3. It reads the screen with you

These run on **read**. What you want to ask is never *what happens next* — it's *what did that just mean*.

| Game | Year | Why it's worth playing this way |
|---|---|---|
| Planescape: Torment | 1999 | A word count that was astronomical in 1999 and has rarely been passed since. Its questions aren't afraid of being discussed — they're starved for someone to discuss them with |
| Umineko: When They Cry | 2007–2010 | It was serialized: the author posed the problem, an internet of readers genuinely worked it for three years, and the next episode answered them. The record of that match survives — you can enter standing on the day the whole world had only read four chapters |
| 428: Shibuya Scramble | 2008 | The first text adventure in Famitsu history to score a perfect 40. Five viewpoints gear into each other, and you constantly want to check "did I just read that right?" — its structure makes every check worth doing with someone |
| Kentucky Route Zero | 2013–2020 | Southern-Gothic magic realism, every act steeped in allusion. What you want to talk about is never what happens next — it's what that last scene actually meant |
| Cultist Simulator | 2018 | It builds the whole experience out of not-understanding, and the wiki will cure you of that — cured, it's gone. What you need is someone who sits in the fog with you without switching on the lights |
| Disco Elysium | 2019 | Concepts, ideologies and references by the screenful; you keep wanting to talk to someone, and nobody around you is on the same page at the same time. And your read on the protagonist rebuilds itself as information arrives — scramble the order and the rebuilding doesn't happen |
| Pentiment | 2022 | Sixteenth-century Bavarian theology, law and gossip. It assumes you know none of it, but every bit you learn makes it glow brighter — what you want on call is a medievalist who also knows exactly how far you've read |

## 4. Series where knowledge flows backward

The damage here doesn't look like a spoiler: prequels, remakes, characters who became memes. You never searched for anything, yet twenty years of series common knowledge has already seen it all for you.

| Game | Year | Why it's worth playing this way |
|---|---|---|
| Kingdom Hearts | 2002 | The series' later complexity is an internet joke, and the memes make you think you need homework to enter. The opposite is true: in 2002 it was clear water, and nobody had done homework. Hold the memes at the door and it becomes the simple thing it was |
| Portal | 2007 | Its lines have circulated as memes for nineteen years; your punchlines have probably been pre-detonated without your knowledge. Putting the memes back in their original places in the script is something only a return to 2007 can do |
| Crisis Core | 2007 | A prequel that assumes you've finished FFVII — played in reverse order, it turns the original's cards face-up one by one. The textbook case of play order itself being a spoiler valve |
| Yakuza 0 | 2015 | A prequel whose co-protagonist's later persona is a world-famous meme. Which is exactly why anyone not yet steeped in series lore holds the rarest thing there is: a pair of 1988 eyes |
| God of War | 2018 | The reverse case: it doesn't need you protected, it needs you to already know the man from 2005. Without that history, every quiet second of it is wasted on you. Here the tool's other half — putting things *into* your head — is the whole job |
| Red Dead Redemption 2 | 2018 | A prequel. Veterans watch it with ten years of memory and every scene gains an echo; newcomers playing this first turn the older game's cards face-up. Play order here isn't a preference — it's a choice, and it should at least be an informed one |
| Final Fantasy VII Remake | 2020 | Rated separately: every trailer and poster for it is a spoiler source for the 1997 original. Original first, remake second — reverse them and both are damaged |
| Metal Gear Solid Δ: Snake Eater | 2025 | Rated separately: the graphics are 2025, but the year to go back to is still 2004. A remake's job is to take you back, not to update you |

## 5. Looks perfect, actually doesn't need it

Not bad games — games where a plain "no spoilers, please" already does the whole job.

| Game | Year | Why it doesn't need this |
|---|---|---|
| Street Fighter II | 1991 | However big its place in history, that history lives in your hands, not in information. What you need is an opponent, not a gatekeeper |
| World of Warcraft | 2004 | Its "back then" isn't the client, it's the server full of people as lost as you. The graphics can be restored — Blizzard restored them itself — the crowd can't. A game this tool can only tell you about, never take you back to |
| To the Moon | 2011 | Pure story with a tear-jerker reputation — looks like the number-one customer. But it's linear, four hours long, and gives you no reason to look anything up midway. One "no spoilers" protects all of it |
| Stardew Valley | 2016 | Its wiki has the status of an official manual; looking things up is part of the culture. There is no ignorance here that needs guarding |
| Hades | 2020 | Its story is dispensed by your death count — the structure is spoiler-proof by design. The designers already did my job for me |
| Baldur's Gate 3 | 2023 | So branching that your version is naturally unlike anyone else's — there's no single correct order to guard. Spoiling one branch spoils a parallel universe, most likely not yours |
